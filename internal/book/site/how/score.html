Maestro

Policies and the score

The queue described three steps that move a line toward a machine. Each step makes a choice, and a policy makes it. An administrator picks the policies, and maestro settings shows the ones in force.

The four policies

SettingThe choice it makesWhen
userWhich of one user's queued lines is that user's nextSelection
selectWhich user's next line is staged, among the users of a groupSelection
allocateWhich of a group's staged lines goes to a machine in the group's turnAllocation
machineWhich of the lines waiting on a machine startsPromotion

All four are fifo until an administrator changes them. Lines then go in the order they were queued, oldest run first, and on a machine the oldest waiting line holds back the others until it fits.

What a policy can be

The first three policies choose before a line is on a machine. At that point there is no free space to fit the line into, so these policies only put lines in an order.

PolicyServes first
fifoThe oldest
lcfsThe newest
siroOne at random. The same seed gives the same choices.
pqThe one with the highest score. Among equal scores, the oldest.

The machine policy can be any of those four, and it also has four that look at the machine's free cores and memory.

PolicyServes first, among the lines that fit now
fifo-skipThe oldest
best-fitThe one that leaves the least free
weighted-best-fitAs best-fit, with the time a line has waited counting in its favour by ω3. A line that fits badly still starts eventually.
tetrisThe one whose cores and memory are most like what the machine has free, so that less of either is stranded (Grandl et al., 2014)

On a machine, fifo and lcfs are strict. They start their first choice if it fits, and start nothing while it doesn't. siro and pq choose among the lines that fit now, as the four policies in the second table do.

The score

pq gives every candidate line a score and serves the highest.

s = ω1·cores + ω2·memory + ω3·waiting + ω4·share

TermWhat it is
coresThe line's cpus, as a fraction of the most cores any machine has
memoryThe line's memory, as a fraction of the most memory any machine has
waitingThe time since the line's run was queued, divided by the horizon. The horizon is an hour unless horizon_minutes says otherwise.
shareThe user's share of the machines, as a fraction of every user's share added together

An administrator sets the four weights, and a weight can be negative.

WeightSettingEffect
ω1cpu_weightBelow 0, lines that ask for few cores go first.
ω2memory_weightBelow 0, lines that ask for little memory go first.
ω3aging_weightAbove 0, a line's score grows while it waits, so no line waits forever.
ω4share_weightBelow 0, a user who holds less of the machines goes first.

A worked example

Take a lab whose largest machine has 16 cores, with select = pq and these weights: ω1 = −0.2, ω2 = 0, ω3 = 0.5 and ω4 = −1. The horizon is an hour. Two users each have a next line.

Ada's lineBo's line
Cores asked for4, so cores = 0.258, so cores = 0.5
Run queued30 minutes ago, so waiting = 0.56 minutes ago, so waiting = 0.1
User's share0.750.25
ω1·cores−0.05−0.10
ω3·waiting+0.25+0.05
ω4·share−0.75−0.25
Score−0.55−0.30

Bo's line has the higher score, so it is staged first. Ada's line has waited longer and is smaller, but Ada already holds three times what Bo holds, and with these weights the share outweighs the rest.

Staging Bo's line raises Bo's share, because a staged line counts as held. The scheduler works the shares out again before it stages the next line.

A user's share

The share setting chooses how a user's share is measured.

MeasureA user's share is
drfThe dominant share now. Take the cores the user's lines hold as a fraction of the cores the machines have for lines, and the same for memory. The share is the larger of the two fractions (Ghodsi et al., 2011).
weighted-drfAs drf, with each fraction divided by the user's weight for that resource. An administrator sets the weights for each user, and a user without any has 1 for both.
assetThe two fractions added together. It is there for comparison only, because it lacks the sharing-incentive property that drf has.
decayThe dominant share of what the user's lines have used in the past. Usage counts half as much after each half-life, which is a week unless half_life_hours says otherwise. Slurm's fair-share works this way.

For drf, weighted-drf and asset, the lines a user holds are those that are staged, assigned, starting or running.

Suppose the machines of the lab in the example have 32 cores and 96 GB for lines in total, and the measure is drf. Ada's lines hold 12 cores and 16 GB. That is 0.375 of the cores and 0.167 of the memory, so her dominant share is 0.375. Bo's lines hold 3 cores and 12 GB, which is 0.094 and 0.125, so his is 0.125. The score uses each as a fraction of the two together, which gives 0.75 for Ada and 0.25 for Bo.

With select = pq, share = drf and ω4 = −1, the queue is the classic fair queue of DRF, in which the user who holds the least goes next.

The limits

SettingWhat it limitsDefault
staged_capacityHow many lines of each group can be staged at onceTwice the number of machines
depthHow many lines can wait on each machine3

A smaller depth leaves the choice of whose line goes next until closer to the moment a machine has room.

When allocation gives a line to a machine, it draws the machine at random among those the line may use that have room in their queue. It reserves nothing on the machine, and promotion decides later whether the line fits. The seed setting makes that draw, and siro, repeat the same choices.
